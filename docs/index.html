The Soap Computer

A soap film pulls itself as small as it can. That one habit makes it solve geometry problems, instantly and beautifully, but only as well as a lazy genius would: it finds a good answer, not always the best one.

a soap film in a wire cube · drag to turn

Every colour here is computed, not painted. The film is a few hundred nanometres of water. Light bouncing off its front and back faces interferes, and each thickness keeps a different set of wavelengths. Switch the light to a sodium lamp, a single yellow wavelength, and the rainbow turns into stripes.

A computer made of soap

Put pins between two glass plates and dip them. The film clings to the pins and pulls its walls as short as possible, so it builds a road network joining the pins. It adds junctions of its own, always three walls at 120°. That is the Steiner tree problem, and for many pins it is famously hard for computers.

Dip a few times with 7 or 10 pins. The film often settles on a network that is locally shortest, where no small wiggle helps, but longer than the best one found by many dips. Sometimes it's even longer than just joining the pins directly. Try the hexagon: the shortest network is just five of the six sides, with no junctions at all, yet the film also settles into networks about 4% and 6% longer. Those are the same three answers real soap gave Dutta, Khastgir and Roy in 2010. The computer scientist Scott Aaronson tried this with glass plates and copper pegs. With a few pegs the film found the best network, but with more pegs it often didn't.

Only two ways to meet

Dip a wire frame and the films meet along lines and at points. In 1873 the Belgian physicist Joseph Plateau set down the rules. By then he had been blind for thirty years, and others dipped the frames and told him what they saw. Films only ever meet three at a time, at 120°, and those lines only ever meet four at a time, at about 109.5°, the angle from the centre of a tetrahedron to its corners. In 1976 the mathematician Jean Taylor proved that for area-minimising films these are the only possibilities. Of the ten cone-shaped ways films can balance, only three are stable: a single flat film, three films along a line, and this four-line point.

Nothing here is told to make 120° or 109.5°. The simulation only moves the film downhill in area. The wires hold their edges, and everything else is free. The angles appear by themselves, because three equal pulls only balance at 120°. In the cube, the little central square saves only 0.07% of area over films meeting in a single point at the centre, yet that point never forms: it isn't one of the two allowed junctions.

A hanging chain, spun

Stretch a film between two rings and it pinches into a waist: a catenoid. Its profile is the curve of a hanging chain, the catenary. Both are shapes that minimise: the chain its height, the film its area. Pull the rings apart and the waist narrows until, at a separation of about 1.33 ring radii, no catenoid exists. The film collapses into two flat disks.

separation h/R

Between h/R = 1.055 and 1.325, the catenoid has more area than the two disks, yet the film stays: a local minimum it can't leave without first growing. Past 1.325 there is no catenoid at all, and it snaps.

Why bubbles swirl like Jupiter

Watch a bubble for a while and its colours drift in slow, round storms. A soap film is a two-dimensional fluid: thousands of times thinner than it is wide, it can only flow sideways within its sheet. In two dimensions, small eddies tend to merge into larger, long-lived vortices rather than break apart, the physics behind Jupiter's storms. The film's thickness barely diffuses, so the flow stretches it into fine threads and winds them into spirals, and the colours show every one.

What stirs it here: the plate warms the foot of the bubble, and warm film is lighter, so it rises toward the top in plumes. Fresh, thick film is drawn up from the wet plate while the top drains thinner. Turn the warmth down and the storms slowly die away. Spin the plate and the film feels a Coriolis force, as a planet's atmosphere does: plumes curl into isolated, hurricane-like vortices with calm eyes, which is what Hamid Kellay's group in Bordeaux saw on heated, rotating half-bubbles.

This is a simulation: an incompressible 2D flow on the bubble's surface, with buoyancy, carrying temperature and thickness. The colours come from the thickness.

Colours, and a sodium lamp

Hold a film upright in a frame and it drains. Water slides down, so the film is thick at the bottom and thin at the top, and the colours sort themselves into bands. Near the frame, thin film is pulled out of the edges. Being lighter, it rises in plumes and curls into swirls. Where the film gets thinner than a few tens of nanometres, the two reflections cancel and it turns black, just before it pops.

Under a sodium lamp there is only one wavelength, 589 nm. Each spot is simply bright or dark. Going from one dark stripe to the next, the film gets thicker by half a wavelength inside water, about 221 nm. Count the stripes and you have measured the film.
